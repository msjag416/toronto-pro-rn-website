Breast Implant Revision · Patient Discussion

Going smaller the second time around? Who's downsized their breast implants or had a revision, and why?

JoAnne Gomez, RN

27+ years ICU · 14+ years plastic surgery recovery · October 2026

Remember when bigger breast implants were the dream? Well, some women are now looking at the implants they chose years ago and thinking, “Yeah, these aren't really me anymore.”

And honestly, that makes sense. The implants you loved at 25 might not be what you want at 38, 45, or 55. Maybe you've had children, gained or lost weight, or gone through menopause. Your body looks different, your lifestyle has changed, and those 400cc implants you once loved might now feel like a bit much.

Apparently, quite a few people are reconsidering their implants. According to RealSelf's Q1 2026 report, pageviews for breast implant revision were up 89% compared with the previous quarter. That measures interest in information about revision surgery, not the number of procedures performed. Some women want a smaller size, while others are considering replacing or removing their implants. So, what's making women reconsider their implants?

Why are women having breast implant revisions?

1. The implants feel too big now.

What looked and felt right years ago might be uncomfortable today. Larger implants can get in the way when you're exercising, buying clothes, or trying to find a comfortable sleeping position. Some women say they no longer want the size or appearance they originally chose. Others simply prefer a smaller breast size that feels more in proportion with their body. That's a personal decision, and you don't need to have a complication to discuss downsizing with a plastic surgeon.

2. Your breasts have become hard or painful.

Scar tissue normally forms around a breast implant. Sometimes that tissue tightens around the implant, a condition called capsular contracture. A breast can feel firm, become painful, or look distorted, especially in more severe cases. A revision may involve removing or releasing scar tissue and replacing or removing the implant. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) describes capsular contracture and other implant complications in its patient information.

3. Your implants have moved out of place.

An implant can move lower, farther to the side, or into a position that causes the breasts to look uneven. The surrounding skin and the implant pocket can also change over time. Weight changes, pregnancy, aging, and the size of the original implants may affect breast appearance. Revision surgery may involve correcting the implant pocket, changing implant size, or combining the procedure with a breast lift. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons includes implant repositioning among the reasons for breast implant revision.

4. One of your implants has ruptured.

A saline implant often becomes noticeably smaller when it leaks. Silicone implant ruptures can be harder to recognize because they may cause no obvious symptoms. If you notice a change in breast shape, pain, swelling, or a new lump, arrange an assessment instead of assuming it's normal aging. Imaging may be needed to evaluate a suspected silicone implant rupture. The FDA explains the differences between saline and silicone ruptures and the possible need for additional surgery.

Thinking about downsizing? There's something you should know.

Suppose you currently have 450cc implants and want to go down to 250cc. It sounds like a simple exchange, but your skin and the implant pocket have already adapted to the larger implants. A smaller implant may not fill the existing space in the same way. Depending on your breast tissue and skin elasticity, you could be left with loose skin, sagging, or nipples that sit lower than you'd like. The original implant pocket might also be too large for the replacement implants. That's why your surgeon may recommend a breast lift or pocket repair along with the exchange. Choosing a smaller cc number won't automatically guarantee smaller, lifted breasts.

Your surgeon will also consider breast width, your natural tissue, skin quality, and the amount of change you want. Two people with the same implant volume can end up with very different results. Ask to see revision photographs of patients who started with a similar implant size and wanted a similar reduction. It's worth discussing the expected shape and scars as carefully as the new implant size.

What kind of surgery might you need?

Implant exchange

Your surgeon removes the current implants and replaces them with smaller implants or implants with a different shape or profile. Sometimes an exchange alone is enough, but the condition of the surrounding tissue determines whether other procedures are needed.

Breast lift (mastopexy)

If your skin has stretched or your nipples are sitting lower, a lift may be recommended at the same time as the exchange. A lift removes excess skin, reshapes the breast, and can reposition the nipples. A lift also creates additional incisions and scars, so it's important to discuss those before deciding.

Pocket repair (capsulorrhaphy)

If the existing implant pocket is too large or the implant has moved, a surgeon can use internal sutures to tighten or reshape the pocket. This may help a smaller implant sit in a better position. The operation is tailored to the individual breast rather than following one standard technique.

Scar tissue removal (capsulectomy)

When capsular contracture or another medical concern is present, your surgeon may remove some or all of the capsule surrounding an implant. Not everyone needs a capsulectomy, and removing the capsule can make surgery more extensive. Discuss the reason for removing it and the risks involved. The FDA outlines these options for implant removal and revision.

Implant removal (explant)

Some women decide they no longer want breast implants. Removing them without replacement is an option, although the breasts may look flatter or have loose skin afterward. A lift may be performed during the same operation or at another time. Cleveland Clinic's patient guide explains the procedure and the possible changes in breast shape after removal.

What's recovery like the second time around?

So you've already experienced breast augmentation recovery, but a revision might be different this time around. The amount of discomfort and the time needed to recover depend on whether you're only exchanging implants or also having a breast lift, pocket repair, or capsule surgery. You may need to wear a supportive surgical bra, and some operations require drains. Your surgeon will give you instructions for lifting, driving, showering, sleeping positions, and wound care. Some people return to desk work within one or two weeks, but that isn't a guaranteed timeline. Soreness can last for weeks, while swelling and breast shape may continue changing for months.

And yes, you'll have to temporarily give up your usual gym and pilates workouts. Strenuous exercise, heavy lifting, and upper-body workouts are generally restricted until your surgeon clears you. For guidance on postoperative activity restrictions and possible drains, see Cleveland Clinic's breast implant removal and recovery information. Follow the instructions for your particular operation rather than comparing your recovery with someone who had a simpler implant exchange.

Contact your surgical team promptly for fever, spreading redness, worsening pain, foul-smelling drainage, sudden swelling, or one breast becoming noticeably larger. If you develop chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or fainting, call 911. These are reasons to seek medical assessment rather than waiting to see whether symptoms improve.
