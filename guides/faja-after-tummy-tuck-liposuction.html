Compression Garments & Fajas

How Long Should You Wear a Faja After a Tummy Tuck or Liposuction? And When Can You Finally Take It Off?

JoAnne Gomez, RN

27+ years ICU · 14+ years plastic surgery recovery · November 2026

If you’ve had a tummy tuck, liposuction 360, or a Brazilian butt lift (BBL), you’ve probably become familiar with the compression garment commonly called a faja. Some patients are told to wear theirs for six weeks, while others are instructed to keep wearing it for three months or longer. Then there are people on Reddit who say they’ve practically lived in theirs for six months! So why do plastic surgeons recommend compression garments, and does wearing one longer improve your surgical results? When can you remove it to shower, sleep, go out for dinner, or simply give yourself a break? And what’s the difference between those Stage 1 and Stage 2 fajas everyone talks about?

Why Do You Need to Wear a Faja After Plastic Surgery?

Following a tummy tuck or liposuction, your body experiences inflammation, swelling, and fluid accumulation as part of the healing process. Liposuction disrupts small blood vessels and lymphatic channels, while a tummy tuck involves separating and repositioning abdominal tissues. A properly fitted compression garment applies gentle, consistent pressure over the operated areas to help manage postoperative swelling. It also provides physical support when you’re walking, getting out of bed, or moving around during those first uncomfortable weeks. Some patients find that this support reduces the pulling sensation around their incisions and makes everyday movement more comfortable. The American Society of Plastic Surgeons recommends compression garments as part of postoperative care, but the evidence that they prevent fluid collections or improve final cosmetic results is limited. That’s worth understanding because wearing a tighter garment or extending compression for months doesn’t necessarily produce better results.

How Long Should You Wear Your Faja?

For liposuction patients, a common recommendation is approximately four to six weeks of compression, although individual surgical instructions vary. Following a tummy tuck, surgeons may recommend several weeks of continuous compression, sometimes extending to six or eight weeks depending on the operation and recovery. During the early postoperative period, some surgeons recommend wearing the garment approximately 23 hours a day, removing it only for approved bathing and garment changes. Others use different schedules, particularly when surgical drains, extensive liposuction, or additional procedures are involved. After the initial weeks, your surgeon may permit longer periods without compression, reduce use to daytime only, or recommend discontinuing it altogether. The important point is that six weeks isn’t a universal stopping date, and six months of compression isn’t medically necessary for every patient. Your surgeon should explain the reason for your prescribed duration and whether continued compression is providing a benefit.

When Can You Remove Your Faja, and for How Long?

During the first postoperative days, you should only remove your faja when your surgeon has permitted it, particularly if you have fresh incisions or surgical drains. Once you’re cleared to shower, it’s generally reasonable to remove the garment for bathing, skin inspection, and changing into a clean garment, unless you’ve received different instructions. If your prescription is to wear compression for 23 hours a day, that means your total time without the garment should generally remain within the permitted hour. Dry your skin carefully, check your incisions, and put the garment back on according to your surgeon’s instructions. Later in recovery, you may be allowed to leave it off for several hours, but that doesn’t mean everyone should immediately stop wearing it overnight. Ask your surgeon specifically when you can begin taking longer breaks and whether you should resume wearing it before bedtime. And please don’t wear a damp, dirty faja because you’ve been told to keep it on around the clock!

Can you manage all of this alone after a tummy tuck, liposuction 360 or BBL? No. Taking a faja off and putting it back on, checking your incisions, and standing up after a shower are hard to do safely by yourself in the first days. Don’t plan to recover alone. Have someone with you during the early postoperative period.

Should You Sleep in Your Faja?

This is another question patients ask frequently, especially when they’re tired of sleeping in tight compression garments. If your surgeon has prescribed around-the-clock compression, that generally includes sleeping in your faja, apart from permitted removal periods. During the first few weeks, nighttime compression may provide support and help manage swelling while your tissues are healing. As your recovery progresses, your surgeon may allow you to sleep without it while continuing daytime compression, or the reverse. There’s no established medical rule that everyone must sleep in a faja for six weeks, three months, or longer. If your surgeon has cleared you to stop using compression completely, you don’t need to continue sleeping in one simply because other patients on Reddit do. Persistent discomfort, breathing restriction, or pressure on your incisions should prompt a discussion about fit rather than forcing yourself to tolerate the garment.

Stage 1 vs. Stage 2 Fajas: What’s the Difference?

If you’ve been shopping for postoperative compression garments, you’ve probably noticed that manufacturers sell Stage 1 and Stage 2 fajas, sometimes with very different designs. Stage 1 garments are generally softer, more flexible, and easier to adjust while your swelling is significant during the early postoperative period. They may have adjustable hooks, zippers, or openings that make bathroom visits easier, particularly when bending is uncomfortable. Stage 2 garments are generally firmer and more structured, often providing a closer fit as postoperative swelling decreases. Some surgeons recommend moving into a Stage 2 garment after the first few weeks, while others prefer patients to continue wearing the original garment throughout recovery. These stage labels are commercial descriptions rather than standardized medical classifications, so a Stage 2 garment isn’t automatically better or medically required. If your faja has become loose because your swelling has decreased, ask your surgeon whether you need a smaller size or a different style rather than purchasing the tightest garment available.

Which Type of Faja Is Best for a Tummy Tuck, Lipo 360, or BBL?

For tummy tuck patients, an abdominal binder or a high-waisted garment may be recommended, depending on the surgeon’s preference and the extent of surgery. A binder is generally easier to adjust, while a full-body garment can provide more consistent coverage when multiple areas have been treated. Patients recovering from liposuction 360 may need compression extending around the abdomen, waist, flanks, and back, sometimes with coverage over the thighs. A Brazilian butt lift requires particular attention because compression should support the liposuctioned areas without creating unwanted pressure over the newly grafted fat in the buttocks. Some BBL garments are designed with reduced compression or specialized panels over the buttocks, but the design should be approved by the operating surgeon. There are also garments with built-in bras, open-crotch designs, and adjustable shoulder straps for patients recovering from combined procedures. The best faja is the compression garment that fits your particular surgery, provides the prescribed compression, and is approved of by your surgeon.

Can a Faja Be Too Tight?

Absolutely, and tighter compression doesn’t necessarily mean better compression! A postoperative garment should fit snugly without causing significant pain, numbness, tingling, restricted breathing, or skin damage. If your faja rolls down, bunches around your waist, or creates deep pressure lines, it may be the wrong size or design. Excessive pressure over an abdominal incision or a recently grafted area can also be a concern, especially following combined procedures. Some patients use foam pads or lipo boards to distribute pressure, but these aren’t universally recommended and haven’t been proven necessary for everyone. Keep checking the skin beneath your garment, particularly around incisions, folds, and areas where the garment fastens. If something feels excessively tight or painful, contact your surgical team rather than assuming discomfort means the faja is working.

When Can You Stop Wearing Your Faja Completely?

As your swelling decreases and your tissues heal, there should eventually be a point when compression is no longer required. Some patients are cleared to stop wearing their garments around four to six weeks, while others continue longer because of the extent of their surgery or persistent swelling. A surgeon may also recommend occasional compression for comfort when swelling fluctuates with activity. However, there’s insufficient scientific evidence that continuing to wear a faja for months will permanently produce a smaller waist or improve the final contour of every tummy tuck or liposuction patient. Once your surgeon has cleared you to discontinue it, you generally don’t need to put it back on after every shower, dinner, or night’s sleep. If you experience new, localized, increasing, or unusual swelling after removing your garment, contact your surgeon rather than assuming you simply need stronger compression. Your body will continue healing and changing for months, whether or not you’re still wearing a faja.

What Has Your Experience Been?

I’ve noticed considerable differences in postoperative compression instructions, even among patients who’ve had similar procedures. Some people say they couldn’t wait to stop wearing their fajas, while others feel more comfortable keeping them on long after their surgeon has cleared them to stop. I’m especially interested in hearing how surgeons explain these differences and whether patients find that compression makes a noticeable difference in swelling and comfort. Did you switch from Stage 1 to Stage 2, and were you allowed to remove it for several hours or sleep without it before your six-week appointment? Did your surgeon recommend compression boards or foam, and did you find them helpful?
