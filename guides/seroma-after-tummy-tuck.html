Seroma After a Tummy Tuck: Symptoms, Treatment, and Recovery

What a fluid-filled swelling feels like after abdominoplasty, why it happens, and when to call your plastic surgeon.

JoAnne Gomez, RN

27+ years ICU · 14+ years plastic surgery recovery · November 2026

You've had your tummy tuck, your surgical drains have been removed, and now you've noticed a new bulge above the incision. When you touch it gently, the area feels soft, almost as if fluid is moving under the skin. You expected some swelling after surgery, but this is different from the tight, swollen abdomen you had during the first few days. After paying for a flatter stomach, finding a new lump isn't especially welcome.

The swelling could be a seroma, a collection of fluid that develops under the skin after an operation. Seromas are a recognized complication of abdominoplasty, and they can appear after the drains come out. Some disappear as the body absorbs the fluid. Others need to be drained by a medical professional. If you notice a new lump, an area that is getting larger, or swelling that concerns you, call your plastic surgeon. A seroma can't be diagnosed from appearance alone, and bleeding or infection can cause similar symptoms.

What Is a Seroma After a Tummy Tuck?

A seroma is a pocket of serous fluid, usually clear or pale yellow, that collects in the area where tissue was separated during surgery. The fluid comes from tiny blood vessels and lymphatic vessels damaged during the operation. During a tummy tuck, the surgeon lifts the abdominal skin and fatty tissue away from the underlying abdominal wall, removes excess tissue, and pulls the remaining skin into position. Some procedures also include repair of separated abdominal muscles. As the tissues heal, fluid can collect in the space between the skin flap and the abdominal wall. When the body produces fluid faster than it absorbs it, a seroma develops.

Seromas are among the recognized postoperative complications of tummy tuck surgery. The chance of developing one depends on the extent of surgery and the techniques used. A seroma doesn't automatically mean that the surgeon did something wrong, and it doesn't mean the operation has failed. It does mean the fluid collection needs to be assessed, especially if it's growing or causing discomfort.

What Does a Seroma Feel Like After a Tummy Tuck?

A seroma can feel like a soft, squishy lump under the skin. Some patients describe a sensation of water moving when they change position or press gently near the swelling. The bulge can appear just above the incision, in the lower abdomen, or more noticeably on one side. There might be tenderness, tightness, or a pulling sensation near the wound. Other patients have little pain, particularly because areas of the abdomen can remain numb for weeks or months after surgery.

A seroma doesn't have to make the skin red or feel hot. The area can look normal except for the new swelling. You can't confirm that a lump contains fluid just by touching it, however. Your surgeon can examine the abdomen and use ultrasound if the diagnosis is uncertain.

Seroma or Normal Swelling: How Can You Tell?

Some swelling is expected after abdominoplasty. During the first few weeks, the abdomen can feel firm, tight, and swollen across a broad area. Swelling tends to improve with time, although residual swelling can last several months. A seroma is more likely to create a distinct bulge or an area that feels fluid-filled. Patients sometimes notice it after the rest of the swelling has started to decrease.

A hematoma, which is a collection of blood, can also cause a lump after surgery. It can develop quickly and be accompanied by increasing pain, bruising, or tightness. An infection can cause swelling along with warmth, redness, fever, and worsening pain. Because these conditions can look similar at first, your surgeon needs to examine any new or enlarging bulge.

When Does a Seroma Appear After a Tummy Tuck?

Seroma symptoms commonly become noticeable about seven to ten days after surgery, although a collection can appear earlier or several weeks later. Some patients first notice swelling after their drains are removed. The drains remove fluid during the early recovery period, but they don't stop the tissues from producing it. Fluid can continue to collect after the tubes are taken out. The drains have been removed; the body hasn't necessarily finished producing fluid.

If a new lump appears months after surgery, don't assume it's a seroma. Scar tissue, a hernia, or another problem can also produce a bulge. Have the area examined.

How Long Does a Seroma Last After a Tummy Tuck?

There's no single recovery time. Some fluid collections shrink within a week or two, and others take several weeks. A larger seroma can last six weeks or longer, particularly if it continues to refill. The amount of fluid, the condition of the tissue, and the treatment chosen all affect the timeline.

Watch for a change in size, discomfort, or the appearance of the skin. A collection that keeps growing, becomes painful, or isn't improving needs to be reassessed by your surgeon. Don't wait for your next routine appointment if the swelling is increasing.

Will a Seroma Go Away on Its Own?

Yes, some seromas disappear without a procedure. The body can gradually absorb a collection that is limited in size and isn't causing pain or pulling on the incision. In those cases, the surgeon can decide to monitor it and arrange a follow-up examination.

Larger collections can require drainage. A persistent seroma can interfere with wound healing, contribute to the incision opening, or become infected. A seroma that keeps returning can eventually develop a thick scar-tissue capsule around the fluid. This is called a chronic seroma or pseudocyst, and surgery is sometimes needed to remove it.

The appropriate treatment depends on what your surgeon finds during the examination. You shouldn't try to decide whether to drain a lump yourself.

How Is a Seroma Treated After a Tummy Tuck?

A surgeon might recommend observation when the collection is limited and the incision is healing well. If the swelling is larger, uncomfortable, or pulling on the wound, the surgeon can recommend needle aspiration. During this procedure, a clinician cleans the skin, inserts a sterile needle into the fluid pocket, and withdraws the fluid with a syringe. Ultrasound can help locate the collection if its position isn't clear.

Aspiration is generally performed in a clinic or medical office. You can feel a pinch or some discomfort, although reduced sensation after surgery can make the procedure less noticeable. The fluid can return, so another aspiration or a temporary drain is sometimes needed. Each needle procedure carries a risk of introducing bacteria, which is one reason surgeons don't automatically drain every collection.

Can You Drain a Seroma at Home?

No. Don't puncture it with a needle, squeeze it repeatedly, or try to force the fluid out through the incision. You can cause bleeding, damage healing tissue, or introduce an infection. Contact your surgeon and describe when the swelling appeared, whether it's getting larger, and whether you have pain, redness, fever, or drainage.

Can a Compression Garment Prevent a Seroma?

Patients recovering from tummy tucks are commonly told to wear a compression garment, also called a faja. The garment can feel comfortable and is part of the postoperative plan prescribed by some surgeons. But the evidence that it prevents seromas is weak. A 2024 systematic review examined five studies involving 130 patients and found insufficient evidence to confirm a reliable reduction in seroma formation.

If your surgeon prescribed a garment, continue to follow those instructions. Don't tighten it excessively in an attempt to flatten a fluid pocket. Excessive compression can make breathing uncomfortable and irritate the healing abdomen. If the garment no longer fits properly because of swelling, call your surgical team before changing how you wear it.

When Should You Worry About a Seroma?

Contact your plastic surgeon the same day if the swelling is increasing, the area is becoming more painful, the incision begins leaking fluid, or you develop redness or warmth around the wound. A temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher, foul-smelling discharge, or an incision that begins to open also needs prompt medical attention. These symptoms can indicate infection or another postoperative complication.

Seek emergency care for sudden, rapidly increasing abdominal swelling, severe pain, uncontrolled bleeding, or fainting. Call 911 for sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or collapse. New pain or swelling in one calf also needs urgent assessment because it can indicate a blood clot. These symptoms require attention regardless of whether you also have a seroma.

How Can You Reduce the Chance of Getting a Seroma?

Surgeons use several techniques to reduce fluid collections. These include internal sutures that bring tissue layers together, preservation of certain tissue layers, and, in selected procedures, surgical drains. Research on abdominoplasty has found lower seroma rates with progressive-tension or quilting sutures, which reduce the area where fluid can collect.

After surgery, follow your surgeon's instructions for drains, activity, and compression garments. If you go home with drains, empty them as directed and record the amount and appearance of the fluid. Report sudden changes in drainage. Avoid lifting heavy objects and returning to exercise before your surgeon approves it. Take short walks as instructed to encourage circulation, and attend your scheduled follow-up visits.

Even if the incision looks fine, a fluid collection can develop under the skin. Tell your surgeon about a new lump instead of waiting to see how large it gets.

Can a Seroma Affect Your Tummy Tuck Results?

A seroma that resolves or is treated appropriately usually doesn't cause a permanent change to the abdominal contour. A collection that keeps returning can delay healing, stretch the tissues, or contribute to scar formation and wound problems. Your surgeon will assess the swelling and decide whether it needs treatment.

It's frustrating to see a new bulge after surgery, particularly when the whole point of the operation was to flatten the abdomen. Finding out what's causing it is more useful than trying to hide it with tighter compression.

Why Postoperative Nursing Care Matters

As a Registered Nurse with experience in intensive care and plastic surgery recovery, I pay close attention to the things patients can miss when they're recovering from an operation: changes in vital signs, the amount of fluid in a drain, the appearance of the incision, and pain that is getting worse instead of improving. Patients also need clear instructions about what to report and who to call outside office hours.

A recovery nurse can monitor vital signs, care for drains as prescribed, assist with medications within the care plan, and help patients move safely after surgery. Seromas commonly appear after the first night, so follow-up with the plastic surgeon remains necessary even when the initial recovery went well. Nurses can recognize concerning symptoms and contact the surgical team; the treating clinician determines the diagnosis and treatment.

Can you recover alone after a tummy tuck? No. Don't plan to be alone during the initial postoperative period. You'll need someone with you to help.
